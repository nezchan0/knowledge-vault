00The environment

What CartPole actually is

A cart slides on a frictionless rail. A pole is hinged to the top of it — free to rotate, not attached rigidly. You cannot touch the pole. You can only shove the cart left or right, and the pole's job is to not fall over.

It's an inverted pendulum, a genuine control-theory benchmark from decades before machine learning. The pole is unstable by default: leave it alone and it falls. The only way to keep it up is to drive the cart underneath the pole's centre of mass — the same thing you do balancing a broomstick on your palm.

That's the trick that makes it a good next step after Frozen Lake. In Frozen Lake, moving right means you end up one tile right. Here, pushing right makes the cart go right and the pole tip left. The action and the consequence point in opposite directions.

The objective, stated exactly There is no goal tile. The reward is +1 for every single step the pole stays up, and the episode ends the instant it tips past 12° or the cart runs off the rail. So "maximise total reward" and "survive as long as possible" are the same sentence. An episode's score is its length.

Compare that to your Frozen Lake page: there, reward was 0 everywhere and +1 once, at the very end, and the hard part was getting that single reward to propagate backwards. Here reward is dense — you get feedback on literally every step. That part is easier. The hard part has moved somewhere else entirely.

01Baseline

Random actions — the number to beat

What's newNo learning at all. Coin-flip every step. This is Level 1 of your Q-learning page before any Q-values existed — except here we measure it, because we need a floor.

Before any algorithm, you need to know what "doing nothing clever" scores. Run a few hundred episodes of pure coin-flipping and watch the distribution. It's tight and it's bad: almost every episode dies between 8 and 60 steps, and the average sits around 22.

02Discretisation

Forcing infinity into a table

What's newBinning. We chop each of the 4 continuous numbers into a fixed number of slots, so a state becomes a row index again — and your Frozen Lake code becomes usable. Still no learning in this level; we're only building the lookup.

Your Q-table needs Q[state][action]. But state here is (0.0231, -0.4417, 0.0089, 0.2260) — a point in 4D space, and you will never see that exact point twice. Not once in a million episodes.

So we cheat. Pick a range for each variable, chop it into n equal slices, and record which slice the value fell in. Four small integers instead of four real numbers. Those four integers combine into one row number, exactly like your 3×3 grid tiles were numbered 0–8.

The exponent is the problem Adding one more slice to a single variable doesn't add a few rows — it multiplies. Ten bins each across four variables is 10 000 rows. A robot arm with 12 joint angles at ten bins each would need 10¹² rows. This is the curse of dimensionality, and it's not a tuning annoyance, it's a wall.

Notice what binning actually threw away. Every θ inside one slice is now literally the same state to the agent — a pole at 3.9° and a pole at 5.9° are indistinguishable if they share a bin. Meanwhile 5.9° and 6.1° might land in different bins and become two totally unrelated rows that share nothing. Both of those are wrong, in opposite directions. Hold that thought for Level 4.

03Tabular Q-learning

Your Frozen Lake code, unchanged

What's newLearning switches on. α, γ, ε — the same three knobs and the same update line from your Q-learning page. The only difference in the entire algorithm is how the row index is computed.
Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α · [ r + γ · maxa′ Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]

Nothing here is new maths. s is now the row index that came out of Level 2's binning, a is 0 or 1, r is +1, and the TD-error bracket does what it always did: measure the gap between what you observed and what you believed, then close a fraction α of it.

One thing Frozen Lake never showed you: because reward is +1 every step and γ is close to 1, Q-values here grow large — a state that can survive 300 more steps is worth roughly 95, not 1. The Q-value is now literally an estimate of how many more steps I can survive from here.

04Diagnosis

Every row is an island

What's newNothing added. This level takes the table you just trained apart and shows the two structural faults that no amount of tuning can fix.

A Q-table is a dictionary. Q[391] and Q[392] are two unrelated memory slots. Updating one has zero effect on the other. In Frozen Lake that was correct and harmless: tile 3 and tile 4 really are different places, and there's no reason experience should leak between them.

Here it's a catastrophe, because rows 391 and 392 might be the pole tilted 4.1° versus 4.3°. Physically almost the same situation. The right action is almost certainly the same. And the table refuses to let one teach the other.

Each curve is averaged over three seeds, because single runs here are too noisy to read. Watch the coverage column rather than the curves — that is the stat that behaves predictably. It falls off a cliff — near-total coverage at the coarsest setting, under 10% at the finest. By the finest setting, more than ninety percent of the table has never been touched and is still sitting at exactly zero.

That is the squeeze. The coarsest table is fully explored but hits a ceiling around 100 — it physically cannot represent a finer distinction, so it cannot act on one. The finest table could represent a much better policy, but it will never get enough visits to fill in. The middle settings are best, and even they are unstable. There is no bin count that gives you both, and the gap widens exponentially with every variable you add to the state.

The one-sentence diagnosis A table can memorise but it cannot generalise. It has no notion that two states are similar, so every state has to be experienced individually — and with continuous inputs there are always more states than you have lifetimes to visit.

So the fix has to be something that takes the four raw numbers and returns Q-values without a lookup — where similar inputs automatically produce similar outputs, because that's a property of the machine itself rather than something you teach it row by row.

That thing is a function. And a neural network is just a function with adjustable knobs.

05Function approximation

Replace the lookup with a network

What's newThe table is gone. A small neural network — 4 inputs, 64 hidden units, 2 outputs — now predicts Q-values from the raw unbinned state. Everything else stays: same TD-error, same γ, same ε-greedy.

Read these two lines next to each other. That's the whole conceptual jump:

table   q = Q[binIndex(s)][a]   // look it up network q = net(s)[a]         // compute it

The network takes the four raw floats — no binning, nothing thrown away — and outputs two numbers: its estimate of Q for "push left" and Q for "push right". Because it's a smooth function, feeding it 4.1° and 4.3° produces nearly identical outputs automatically. Generalisation stops being something you hope for and becomes free.

Training changes shape too. A table cell is assigned a value. A network is nudged toward one: compute the same TD target r + γ·max Q(s′,·), treat it as the correct answer, and take a gradient step to shrink the error. Instead of α controlling a blend, a learning rate controls the size of that nudge — and the nudge adjusts weights that affect every state at once.

This is DQN What you're about to run is the Deep Q-Network algorithm — the 2013 DeepMind method that learned Atari from pixels. It is Q-learning with the table swapped for a network, plus two stabilisers, both of which are toggles below so you can watch what happens without them.
StabiliserWhat it doesThe problem it fixes
Replay buffer Stores the last 20 000 transitions. Trains on a random batch of 64 drawn from history, not on the step that just happened. Consecutive steps are nearly identical, so training on them in order is like studying one flashcard 50 times in a row. The network overfits to the last few seconds and forgets everything else.
Target network Keeps a frozen copy of the network and uses the copy to compute max Q(s′,·). Syncs it every 300 steps. Otherwise the network chases a target that it itself moves every time it updates. Values spiral upward and diverge — you're chasing your own tail.

The experiment worth running Train with both toggles on and leave it running to at least 40 000–50 000 env steps — roughly 15–25 seconds. Below about 30 000 it looks unconvincing. Watch "best ever" hit 500 before the mean does. Then reset, switch both off, and run again with everything else identical. It collapses to around 10 steps per episode — not merely worse than the network, worse than random, because it has actively learned a broken policy. Those two "hacks" aren't polish, they're what makes the whole thing possible.

One honest caveat: DQN is not guaranteed to converge. Tabular Q-learning has a proof behind it; the moment you combine bootstrapping, off-policy learning and function approximation you lose that guarantee — the trio is known as the deadly triad. Run this five times on identical settings and you may see means of 400, 320, 90, 370 and 100 — that spread is from actual measurements of this exact code, not a caveat for form's sake. Every one of those runs touched 500 at least once. That's not you misconfiguring it, that's the actual state of the field.
