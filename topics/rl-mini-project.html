00 · The idea, in plain words

Nobody tells the agent the right answer

You already know linear regression. There, you hand the model a table: hours studied → marks scored. Every row comes with the correct answer attached. The model's job is to fit a line through answers it has already been shown.

Reinforcement learning has no such table. There is no dataset. There is a game, and a player who starts out pressing buttons at random, and a score. That's it.

The player presses buttons. Sometimes the score goes up. Sometimes the player dies. Slowly — over thousands of games — it works out which buttons to press in which situations. It generates its own training data by playing. Nobody ever tells it "at this moment, jump." It works that out from the fact that jumping tended to be followed, eventually, by points.

Why this matters for your project

When your teacher asks "where is your dataset?", the honest answer is: there isn't one, and that's the point. The environment is the dataset. This is the third branch of ML, alongside supervised (regression, Naive Bayes) and unsupervised (k-means). It is fully on-topic.

The six words you need

Every RL problem, from FrozenLake to a robot arm, is described with the same six words. Learn them now and the rest of this document reads easily.

Agent
The thing that is learning. Your code. In Flappy Bird, the agent is the decision-maker that chooses flap or don't-flap — not the bird sprite itself.
Environment
The world the agent acts in. The game. It receives an action and replies with a new situation and a number. You do not write the environment; you install it.
State
What the agent can see right now. A snapshot. On FrozenLake it's simply "which of the 16 squares am I on". In Flappy Bird you will design it yourself, and that design is the hardest and most interesting decision in your project.
Action
What the agent can do. A choice from a short list. FrozenLake: left, down, right, up. Flappy Bird: flap, or don't. That's the whole list.
Reward
A single number the environment hands back after every action. The only feedback that exists. +1 for reaching the goal, −1 for dying. Not a label, not a correction — just a number.
Episode
One complete play-through, from reset until the agent wins or dies. One game. "Training for 5000 episodes" means it played 5000 games.

The loop that connects them never changes, in any RL program ever written:

look at the state → choose an action → environment returns a reward and a new state → learn from what just happened → repeat until the episode ends

01 · The algorithm

Q-learning is a table of guesses that corrects itself

There are dozens of RL algorithms. You are going to use exactly one: Q-learning. It is the oldest, the simplest, and the only one where you can literally print the model and read it. That last property is worth more to you right now than any amount of performance.

What the model actually is

The entire model is a table. Rows are states. Columns are actions. FrozenLake has 16 squares and 4 moves, so the model is a 16×4 grid of numbers — 64 numbers total.

The number in row s, column a is called Q(s, a), and it means one specific thing:

Definition · commit this to memory

Q(s, a) = "if I am in state s and I take action a, how much total reward do I expect to collect from here until the end of the episode?"

Not the reward for this step alone — the reward for this step plus everything that follows. That's why a square two moves from the goal can have a high value even though standing on it scores nothing.

Once the table is filled in correctly, playing is trivial: look at your current row, pick the column with the biggest number. The hard part is filling it in, and you start with all zeros — sixty-four wrong guesses.

The update rule

After every single action, one cell of the table gets nudged. This is the whole of Q-learning:

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α · [ r + γ · max Q(s',·) − Q(s,a) ]
Q(s,a)the old guess for the action you just took
rthe reward you actually just received
max Q(s',·)the best value in the row of the state you landed in — your estimate of how good your new position is
γdiscount, 0→1. How much future reward is worth compared to immediate reward
αlearning rate, 0→1. How big a step to take toward the new estimate

Read the bracket as one thing. It is the surprise: what I now think this move was worth, minus what I previously thought it was worth. If the surprise is zero, the guess was already right and nothing changes. If it's positive, that move was better than expected, so nudge it up. Alpha decides how far to nudge.

Why does this ever converge? A worked example.

Take FrozenLake, γ = 0.95, α = 1 for simplicity. Everything starts at zero.

Early on, the agent stumbles randomly and mostly gets r = 0. The bracket is 0 + 0.95·0 − 0 = 0. Nothing changes. The table stays flat. This is normal and it is why nothing seems to happen for the first few hundred episodes.

Then it randomly lands on the goal. That step gets r = 1. The bracket becomes 1 + 0 − 0 = 1, so the cell for "move right from square 14" becomes 1.0. One cell is now non-zero.

Next time it happens to reach square 14, the update uses max Q(14) = 1.0. The bracket is 0 + 0.95·1.0 − 0 = 0.95. So the cell leading into 14 becomes 0.95.

Then 0.9025, then 0.857… The value flows backwards from the goal, one square per visit, discounted by γ each step. That's what you are watching when the demo grid lights up from the bottom-right corner outward. Squares far from the goal end up worth less, which is exactly right — they take longer to cash in.

And now γ makes sense: it's the exchange rate between "reward now" and "reward later". At γ = 0.5 the signal halves every step and dies before it travels four squares. At γ = 0.95 it survives the whole board.

Explore or exploit

One problem remains. If the agent always picks the highest number in its row, it will lock onto the first mediocre route it stumbles across and never discover the better one. But if it always moves randomly, it never uses what it learned.

The fix is embarrassingly simple and it is called epsilon-greedy. Keep a number ε between 0 and 1. Before each move, roll a die:

  • with probability ε → explore: pick a completely random action
  • otherwise → exploit: pick the action with the highest Q-value

Start at ε = 1.0 (total beginner, moves at random) and multiply it by something like 0.999 after every episode, with a floor around 0.01. The agent gradually stops guessing and starts playing. Watch the epsilon readout in the demo drop as the board lights up — those two things are causally linked.

The single most common beginner failure

Decaying epsilon too fast. The agent turns greedy at episode 200, before it has ever seen the goal, commits forever to a bad route, and your win rate flatlines at zero. If your agent isn't learning, suspect epsilon before you suspect your update rule.

STAGE 01 · FrozenLake · one evening

Sixteen squares, sixty-four numbers

A 4×4 grid. You start top-left, the goal is bottom-right, and four squares are holes that end the episode instantly. Reward is 1 for reaching the goal and 0 for literally everything else.

Why start here

Because the entire model fits on your screen. You can print(Q) and read all 64 numbers. When something goes wrong you can see exactly which state has the wrong value — a luxury you lose forever after this stage. Do not skip it because it looks too easy. Its job is to prove your update rule is correct, so that when Flappy Bird fails later, you know the bug isn't there.

We start with is_slippery=False, meaning moves are deterministic — press right, go right. The slippery version is a genuinely harder problem and you'll come back to it at the end of the stage.

What you should see

Win rate sits at roughly 0.00–0.05 for the first several hundred episodes while epsilon is high and the agent is basically flailing. Then it climbs, and by around episode 2000–3000 it should be pinned near 1.00. That S-shaped curve is what learning looks like. It is also what the canvas at the top of this page is drawing.

Watch it actually play

Training with graphics on is painfully slow, so you train blind, save the table, then load it and watch. This save-and-replay pattern is standard and you'll reuse it in both later stages.

stage1_watch.py

Before you move on

Don't just run it. Break it deliberately — this is where the actual learning happens, and it gives you real results to write about.

  • Print the table. print(Q.round(3)). Find the row for square 14 (one left of the goal). Column 2 is "right". Is it the biggest? Should be, it steps onto the goal.
  • Set gamma = 0.5 and retrain. The agent goes short-sighted. Note how far the value signal travels from the goal before it dies out.
  • Set epsilon_decay = 0.99 (very fast). Epsilon hits the floor by episode ~500. Record what happens to the win rate and why.
  • Set is_slippery=True. Now each move has a 1/3 chance of going the intended way and 1/3 each perpendicular. Same code still learns, but slower and to a lower ceiling (~0.7–0.8), and you'll want a smaller alpha like 0.1 — with random outcomes, a big alpha means one unlucky slip overwrites everything you knew.

STAGE 02 · CartPole · one evening

The state is a real number. Now what?

Balance a pole upright on a cart by pushing the cart left or right. Reward is +1 for every frame the pole stays up. Episode ends when it tips past ~12° or the cart runs off the screen. CartPole-v1 caps at 500 steps, so 500 is a perfect score.

Why this stage exists

FrozenLake gave you state number 7. CartPole gives you this:

the problem

Four continuous numbers: cart position, cart velocity, pole angle, pole angular velocity. You cannot index a table with 0.0273. And there are infinitely many possible values, so the agent would never see the same state twice — it could never learn anything, because learning requires revisiting a situation and doing better.

Two ways out. The professional answer is a neural network that takes the four floats and outputs Q-values — that's DQN, and it needs a GPU and a week of your life. The answer for this stage is discretisation: chop each continuous number into buckets and treat each bucket-combination as one state.

Angle 0.0273 rad falls in bucket 3. Angle 0.0281 also falls in bucket 3. They're now the same state, so experience gathered in one applies to the other. That's the trick: you deliberately throw away precision in exchange for being able to generalise.

The bucket count tradeoff

Too few buckets: genuinely different situations get merged, and the agent can't tell "pole tipping gently left" from "pole falling hard left". It can never play well — the information simply isn't in its state.

Too many buckets: the table explodes. 20 buckets per variable = 160,000 states. Each needs visiting many times. Training takes forever.

We use 3×3×6×6 = 324 states. Note the asymmetry: 6 buckets for the pole's angle and angular velocity, only 3 for the cart's position and velocity. That's a deliberate modelling choice — the pole is what you're balancing, the cart barely matters. Deciding what your agent needs to see precisely is a design decision, and it's exactly the skill Stage 03 will demand of you.

stage2_cartpole.py

What you should see

Around 20 steps at the start (a pole falls fast when you push randomly). By episode 5000 you should be in the low hundreds; by 15000–20000 you should be regularly hitting 400–500.   • Try BUCKETS = (3, 3, 2, 2). It collapses. Explaining why is a strong paragraph in your write-up.

STAGE 03 · Flappy Bird · the submission

Now you design the state yourself

This is the project you actually submit. Everything before it was preparation, and you'll find that the algorithm barely changes — it's the same update rule you wrote in Stage 01. What changes is that nobody hands you the state any more.

terminal

Step one: look at what you're given

Never write code against an observation you haven't printed. Library versions differ and the ordering of these values can change between releases. Run this first, always:

stage3_inspect.py

With use_lidar=False you should get 12 numbers, all normalised: horizontal and vertical positions for the last, next, and next-next pipe pairs, then the bird's own y position, its vertical velocity, and its rotation.

Verify the indices before trusting them

The code below assumes indices 3, 4, 5 for the next pipe and 9, 10 for the bird. Confirm with the inspect script: let the bird fall and watch which value climbs steadily (that's player_y), and which resets sharply when a pipe is passed (that's the pipe x). If your version differs, change the five constants at the top of the file and nothing else.

Step two: design the state

Twelve numbers is far too many — the table would have more cells than atoms you have patience for. So ask the real question: what does a human actually look at when playing Flappy Bird?

Not the pipes behind you. Not the pipe after next. Three things only:

  • dx — how far ahead the next pipe is. Controls urgency.
  • dy — how far above or below the gap's centre you are. This is the key one: it's not raw position, it's position relative to where you need to be. Bird at y=0.5 means nothing on its own. Bird 0.2 below the gap means flap.
  • vel — how fast you're falling or rising. Without it the agent can't tell a bird that's about to overshoot from one that's about to stall.

Three numbers, bucketed to 15 × 21 × 10 = 3150 states × 2 actions. Small enough to fill by playing, rich enough to play well. This paragraph is the intellectual core of your project — say all of it in your report.

Watch it play

What you should see

This one is slow to start and then goes almost vertical. Expect near-zero pipes for the first few thousand episodes, then a long slow climb, then a fairly sudden takeoff once the agent discovers that hovering near the gap centre pays. Twenty to fifty thousand episodes is normal, and each is short, so it's minutes-to-an-hour on a laptop, not days.

Two notes on the code worth understanding rather than copying. First, the terminal handling: when the bird dies there is no future, so the gamma * max(Q[next]) term must be zeroed out — otherwise the agent credits a dead bird with imaginary future points. FrozenLake tolerated skipping this; here it matters. Second, the flap-frequency line: it's a diagnostic, not a hyperparameter. Wildly high or near-zero flap rates tell you the agent has collapsed onto one action, which usually means epsilon decayed too fast.

If it plateaus at one or two pipes

In order: (1) train longer — seriously, this is the answer most of the time. (2) Widen DY_BINS resolution near zero; distinguishing "slightly below centre" from "on centre" is what precision flying needs. (3) Lower alpha to 0.05 for the last stretch so late learning stops overwriting good values. (4) Only then consider changing the reward.

03 · When it doesn't work

It will not work the first time

That's the normal experience, not a sign you've misunderstood something. RL fails silently — no crash, no error, just a flat line where a learning curve should be. Work through these in order.

The reward never changes. Flat line at zero, forever.

Check the reward is actually arriving: print(reward) inside the loop for one episode. If it's always 0, the agent has never once reached the goal, so there is nothing to learn from — every update computes zero surprise.

Fix: raise epsilon and keep it high longer, or lengthen the step limit per episode. On FrozenLake with is_slippery=True, random walking finds the goal rarely, so it can take a few thousand episodes before the first non-zero reward appears. Patience is a legitimate fix here.

It learns, then gets worse.

Almost always alpha too high, especially in stochastic environments. With α = 0.8 a single unlucky episode overwrites a value you'd built up over hundreds. Drop to 0.1, or decay alpha over time the same way you decay epsilon.

Also check you aren't still exploring at 30% by the end — a good policy with high epsilon looks like a bad policy, because a third of its moves are random. Print epsilon alongside your score.

ValueError: too many values to unpack

You mixed gymnasium's 5-value API with a tutorial's 4-value gym API. Gymnasium: obs, reward, terminated, truncated, info = env.step(a) and obs, info = env.reset(). Old gym: obs, reward, done, info and obs = env.reset(). Pick gymnasium and convert any tutorial code you borrow.

IndexError or TypeError when indexing Q

You're indexing a multi-dimensional array with a list or numpy array instead of a tuple. Q[[1,2,3]] means something entirely different from Q[(1,2,3)]. Always return a tuple(...) from your discretise function — note every example here does.

An out-of-range index means a value fell outside your bin bounds. np.digitize happily returns len(bins) for anything past the last boundary, so size your table as n_buckets where bins has n_buckets − 1 entries, exactly as the helper here does.

It works while training but plays terribly when I watch it

You're not loading the trained table — check the np.load path actually points at the file you saved. Or you're still sampling random actions in the watch script: at playback time there should be no epsilon at all, only np.argmax.

Third possibility: your discretise function differs between the two files. If training bucketed with one set of bounds and playback uses another, every lookup hits the wrong row. Keep the function in one file and import it, or copy it character for character.

Training is unbearably slow

Never pass render_mode="human" during training — drawing frames is hundreds of times slower than simulating them. Train headless, save, then render only for the demo video. Also remove per-step print calls; printing thousands of lines a second genuinely dominates the runtime.

04 · The write-up

What gets you the marks

A working agent is table stakes. What separates a good mini project from a forgettable one is showing that you understood the choices you made. You have three environments of increasing difficulty and a clean narrative running through them — use it.

Plot your learning curves

Every RL report lives or dies on this graph. Save the score list from each run and plot it with a moving average, because raw per-episode scores are far too noisy to read.

plot_curve.py

Structure that works

  1. Problem. What RL is and how it differs from the supervised methods covered in class. Two paragraphs, in your own words.
  2. Algorithm. The Q-table, the update rule, and the meaning of each term. Include the equation. Explain the exploration/exploitation tradeoff.
  3. Stage 01 — FrozenLake. Baseline. Include the printed Q-table and explain why the values increase toward the goal. Compare slippery vs non-slippery.
  4. Stage 02 — CartPole. The discretisation problem and how you solved it. Your bucket-count experiments belong here — they're real ablation results.
  5. Stage 03 — Flappy Bird. The main event. Justify your three-feature state design at length: what you included, what you discarded from the 12 raw values, and why.
  6. Results. Learning curves for all three. A table of final scores. Your hyperparameter comparisons.
  7. Limitations and next steps. Where tabular Q-learning breaks down, and what DQN would change. This section is short, cheap, and disproportionately impressive.

Include your failures

"Epsilon decay of 0.99 caused premature convergence and the agent plateaued at a 12% win rate; slowing it to 0.999 fixed it" is worth more than any successful run. It proves you diagnosed a system rather than copied one. Keep notes as you go — you will not remember these details a week later.

Record a video

Set render_mode="human" in the watch scripts and screen-record thirty seconds of each agent. A clip of the bird clearing pipes does more in a demo than any slide.
