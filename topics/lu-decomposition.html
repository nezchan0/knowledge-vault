Two TrianglesLinear algebra · module
    Systems · Elimination · Elementary matrices · LU · LDU

    Every square matrix wants to be two triangles.

    Solving Ax = b is hard. Solving it when A is triangular is almost free. So the whole game is: take the hard matrix apart into triangles once, and reuse them forever. Here is that idea, built up one move at a time, with everything you can click through yourself.

    01 — the problem

    What a linear system actually is

    A linear equation is one where every unknown appears alone, to the first power, multiplied by a number. No x², no xy, no sin x. That restriction is what makes the whole theory work.

    Three equations in three unknowns:

    The numbers in front of the unknowns form a matrix A. The unknowns stack into a vector x. The right-hand sides stack into b. The entire system is then one statement: Ax = b.

    Why bother rewriting it as a matrix

    Because the unknowns stop mattering. Every operation you would do to the equations — scale one, swap two, subtract one from another — is an operation on rows of numbers. Dropping the letters means you can hand the job to a fixed procedure, and later to a machine.

    Two readings of the same equation

    Row picture
    Intersecting flats

    Each equation is a line (2D) or a plane (3D). A solution is a point lying on all of them at once. Good for seeing how many solutions exist.

    Column picture
    Mixing columns

    Ax is a weighted sum of A's columns. Solving asks: what mix of the columns produces b? Good for seeing why a solution can fail to exist.

    02 — outcomes

    A system has one, none, or infinitely many solutions. Never two.

    Before learning a method, know what the method can return. Two straight lines can cross once, never, or lie on top of each other. There is no fourth option, and that stays true in any number of dimensions.

    Drag the coefficients and watch the two lines respond. The classifier below reports what elimination would find.

    Why this comes first

    Elimination doesn't "fail" on a bad system — it happily produces a row like 0 0 | 5 (impossible, so no solution) or 0 0 | 0 (no information, so infinitely many). If you don't know these outcomes exist, you'll read those rows as a bug in your arithmetic.

    03 — the one move

    Gaussian elimination: subtract rows until the bottom-left is empty

    You are allowed exactly three moves, because each one is reversible and so none of them changes the solution set:

    1. Swap two rows. The equations were never in any order.
    2. Scale a row by a nonzero number. Doubling both sides of an equation changes nothing.
    3. Add a multiple of one row to another. If two statements are both true, so is their combination.

    Move 3 is the workhorse. Pick the first nonzero entry of a row — the pivot — and use it to knock out everything below it in that column. Work left to right. When you're done, the matrix is upper triangular and the last equation has one unknown in it.

    What we gained

    An upper triangular system is solvable by reading it backwards: the bottom row gives you one variable outright, substitute it up one row to get the next, and so on. No searching, no guessing — n divisions and some multiplications.

    04 — the trick

    Elementary matrices: a row operation is just a matrix you multiply by

    Here is the pivot of the whole subject. Take the identity matrix and perform one row operation on it. The result is called an elementary matrix, E. Multiplying E·A performs that same operation on A.

    Build one and watch it act:

    Why turn operations into matrices

    Because matrices compose. A sequence of eight row operations becomes a single product E₈…E₂E₁A = U. And products can be moved across the equals sign by inverting them — which is exactly how the L in LU appears. Without this step, elimination is a procedure. With it, elimination is an equation.

    Every elementary matrix is trivially invertible

    Undoing a row operation is another row operation, so E⁻¹ is another elementary matrix, and you can write it down by inspection — no inversion algorithm needed:

    OperationEInverse operationE⁻¹
    Rᵢ ↔ Rⱼidentity with rows i, j swappedswap them backsame matrix (E⁻¹ = E)
    Rᵢ → c·Rᵢidentity with c at (i,i)divide by cidentity with 1/c at (i,i)
    Rᵢ → Rᵢ − ℓ·Rⱼidentity with −ℓ at (i,j)add it backidentity with +ℓ at (i,j)

    Order matters, and the side matters

    E·A acts on rows; A·E acts on columns. And E₂E₁ ≠ E₁E₂ in general — the elimination order is baked into the product. Read E₃E₂E₁A right to left: E₁ happened first.

    05 — the factorisation

    From elimination to A = LU

    Elimination gave you Eₖ … E₂E₁ A = U. Multiply both sides on the left by the inverses, in reverse order, and everything on the left collapses onto A:

    A = E₁⁻¹ E₂⁻¹ … Eₖ⁻¹ · U = L · U

    That product of inverses is L: lower triangular, ones on the diagonal. And it has a property that feels like cheating.

    The free lunch

    You never multiply those inverse matrices. Each multiplier ℓᵢⱼ you used in "Rᵢ → Rᵢ − ℓᵢⱼ·Rⱼ" simply drops into position (i, j) of L, sign unchanged. The numbers you were already computing and throwing away are L. Recording the factorisation costs nothing beyond the elimination you were doing anyway.

    The machine below is the heart of this page. Step through elimination on the left and watch L fill in on the right — each multiplier physically moves into its slot.

    Why ones on L's diagonal

    Convention, and it makes the factorisation unique. All the scaling information is left inside U's pivots. Section 08 pulls that scaling out into its own matrix, which is where LDU comes from.

    06 — the payoff

    Solving with L and U: two easy sweeps

    Now Ax = b becomes L(Ux) = b. Name the inner part y = Ux and you have two triangular systems, each solvable by substitution:

    1. Forward substitution — solve Ly = b downwards, starting from the top row.
    2. Back substitution — solve Ux = y upwards, starting from the bottom row.

    Step through both, one arithmetic line at a time. Change b and the solve re-runs against the same L and U — which is the entire point.

    What we gained, in numbers

    Factoring costs roughly ⅔n³ operations. Each solve afterwards costs about 2n². For n = 500 and 100 right-hand sides, redoing elimination each time costs about 8.3 billion operations; factoring once and substituting costs about 133 million. Same answers, roughly 60× less work — and the gap widens with every extra b.

    07 — when it breaks

    A zero pivot, and the P in PA = LU

    Elimination assumes you can divide by the pivot. If the pivot is zero, you can't. Two different things may have happened, and they need different responses:

    Fixable
    A nonzero sits below

    Swap the rows and carry on. Record the swap in a permutation matrix P. The result is PA = LU — the factorisation of a re-ordered A.

    Not fixable
    The whole column below is zero

    No swap helps. That column contributes no pivot: A is singular, det A = 0, and Ax = b has either no solution or infinitely many.

    Try the presets Needs a row swap and Singular in the machine above to watch both happen live.

    In real software, pivots swap even when they're not zero

    A pivot of 0.0000001 is legal but disastrous: dividing by it produces enormous multipliers and destroys precision. Library code (LAPACK's dgetrf, NumPy's scipy.linalg.lu) uses partial pivoting — always move the largest available entry in the column into the pivot seat. It's for numerical stability, not correctness.

    08 — the refinement

    LDU: separating the scale from the shape

    Look at the U you produced. Its diagonal holds the pivots, and they're carrying two jobs at once: shape (which variables couple to which) and scale (how big each pivot is). Pull the scale out into a diagonal matrix D, and divide each row of U by its pivot:

    A = L · D · U′   where D = diag(pivots), U′ = D⁻¹U

    Now L and U′ both have ones on the diagonal, and every scaling factor lives in one obvious place.

    What LDU buys you

    Uniqueness — for an invertible A with no swaps needed, L, D and U′ are the only such matrices. Determinant by inspection — det A = product of D's diagonal. Symmetry becomes visible — if A = Aᵀ then U′ = Lᵀ exactly, which halves both the work and the storage.

    The symmetric case: A = LDLᵀ, and Cholesky

    When A is symmetric, the factorisation folds in half: A = LDLᵀ. If additionally every pivot is positive (A is positive definite), you can push the square root of D into both sides and get A = R Rᵀ — the Cholesky factorisation, twice as fast as LU and unconditionally stable. This is what actually runs inside least-squares fitting, Gaussian processes, and Kalman filters.

    10 — in the wild

    Where this is actually used

    LU is not a classroom exercise that gets replaced later. It is the default dense linear solver in essentially every numerical stack — numpy.linalg.solve, MATLAB's backslash, Eigen, LAPACK — all of them factor and then substitute.
